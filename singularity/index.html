01  CompactnessSame mass. Smaller radius.

Squeeze a star
until light bends.

Take the Sun and shrink it, keeping its mass. At its real size, gravity bends starlight passing its edge by under two arcseconds, the shift Eddington measured during the 1919 eclipse. Compress it to a few thousand kilometres and the bending becomes visible: stars behind it slide outward and smear into arcs.

Squeeze further and a background star sitting exactly behind becomes an Einstein ring. The surface itself distorts; light from the far side is bent around to the front so you see the back of the star past its own edges. Its colour drains to red as light climbs out of the deepening well.

At 100% compactness, nothing on the surface can leave. The sphere where that happens is the event horizon.

02  Observing the horizonFrom a safe distance

You never see
anything fall in.

Watch a star collapse. Its surface races inward, but the light leaving it has to climb an ever steeper well. Each photon arrives later and redder than the last. The surface slows in your view, stalls at the horizon, and fades. It does not vanish; it dims toward black over a handful of microseconds, centre first, edges last.

The dark disc you then see is not the horizon. It is the shadow, about 2.6 times wider than the horizon, bounded by the photon ring: a thin bright line where light has circled the hole once or many times before reaching you. Just outside it the whole background sky is folded into a lensed halo; every star appears twice, once on each side.

Drop an apple in. It slows, freezes against the dark, turns red, and fades. On your clock it never crosses. On its own, it already has.

03  Crossing the horizonFrom the inside

Nothing marks
the line.

Fall in yourself and the horizon is not a surface you hit. Locally, spacetime looks ordinary. No wall, no flash, no alarm; you cross in finite time on your own watch, and if the hole is large enough you feel nothing at all.

What changes is the sky. The universe outside does not disappear. It gathers behind you into a shrinking, brightening disc, rimmed by the photon ring, while the black region swells to fill everything ahead. Light from outside still reaches you; it is only your light that cannot go back.

Inside, the radial direction becomes time. The singularity is not a place at the centre you could steer around. It is a moment in your future, as unavoidable as next Tuesday. Nearer to it, the difference in pull between your head and feet grows without limit: you are stretched along the fall and squeezed across it.

The sky disc narrows to a point. Then there is no then.

The outside universe, seen from inside: a shrinking disc of sky behind you.
