01  What a black hole isA region, not an object

Gravity with
no way out.

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is strong enough that nothing, not even light, can leave. The boundary of that region is the event horizon. It is not a surface you could touch. It is the set of points from which no signal can ever reach the outside; a line on a map, not a wall.

For a non-spinning mass M, the horizon sits at the Schwarzschild radius:

rs = 2GM / c2

It scales with mass alone. Double the mass, double the radius. The density needed to make a black hole falls as the mass grows: a stellar one is denser than an atomic nucleus, while a supermassive one can be less dense than water.

Anything compressed inside its own Schwarzschild radius becomes a black hole. For the Sun that radius is 3 km. For Earth, 9 mm.

02  AnatomyFrom the outside in

Rings around
a hole.

Accretion disc

Infalling gas spirals in and heats by friction to millions of degrees, shining in X-rays. Most black holes we detect, we detect this way. A black hole with nothing falling in is dark.

Innermost stable circular orbit · 3 rs

Inside this radius no stable orbit exists; matter plunges. The inner edge of the disc sits roughly here.

Photon sphere · 1.5 rs

Light can orbit, unstably. Light that grazes it loops once or many times before escaping, producing the thin bright photon ring seen in images.

Shadow · about 2.6 rs

What a distant camera sees as dark is larger than the horizon, because lensing bends rays from behind the hole around it. The shadow is the horizon's magnified silhouette.

Event horizon · 1 rs

The point of no return. Locally, nothing marks it.

Singularity

Where general relativity predicts infinite curvature and its own equations stop. Most physicists expect quantum gravity to replace this with something else. Nobody yet knows what.

Spinning black holes (nearly all real ones) add an ergosphere outside the horizon, where spacetime itself is dragged around faster than light can resist. Energy can be extracted from it.
Radii for a non-spinning (Schwarzschild) black hole, to scale with each other.
03  How they formThree routes

When pressure
loses.

Stellar collapse

A star holds itself up against gravity with the pressure of fusion. When a massive star (roughly 20 solar masses or more at birth) exhausts its fuel, the core collapses. Below about 1.4 M☉ electron pressure stops it: a white dwarf. Below about 2 to 3 M☉ neutron pressure stops it: a neutron star. Above that nothing known can. The core falls through its own horizon in under a second. The outer layers rebound as a supernova, or in some cases fall in silently.

Supermassive growth

The millions-to-billions solar mass black holes at galaxy centres grew by swallowing gas and merging with each other over billions of years. How they got so large so early in the universe's history is an open problem; some may have started from the direct collapse of enormous gas clouds.

Primordial

Density fluctuations in the first second after the Big Bang might have produced black holes of almost any mass. None has been confirmed. If they exist, small ones would be evaporating now (see 05).

Spacetime diagram of a collapse. The horizon (red) forms inside the star before the surface reaches it; the singularity (pink) lies in the future of everything inside.
04  What you would seeOutside, and inside

Frozen from here.
Finite from there.

From outside, anything falling toward the horizon appears to slow down, turn red, and fade. Light climbing out of the gravitational well loses energy (gravitational redshift) and arrives ever later (time dilation). You never see the crossing. The infalling object dims to black in a fraction of a millisecond without ever quite disappearing.

From inside, the horizon is uneventful. You cross it in finite time on your own clock, feeling nothing in particular if the hole is large. The outside universe does not go dark; it shrinks into a bright disc behind you. Inside, the radial direction becomes time-like: the singularity is not a place you could avoid but a moment in your future.

Near a stellar-mass hole, tidal forces (the difference in gravity between your head and feet) would tear you apart well before the horizon. Near a supermassive one you would pass the horizon intact and have hours before the end.

A collapsing surface as seen from outside: frozen at the horizon, redshifting toward black.
05  Hawking radiationBlack holes are not quite black

They glow.
Barely.

In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed that when quantum field theory is applied near an event horizon, a black hole must emit thermal radiation. The popular picture: pairs of virtual particles constantly appear and vanish in empty space; near the horizon one partner can fall in while the other escapes, carrying energy away. The picture is a heuristic; the real calculation is about how the vacuum looks different to observers near the horizon and far from it. But the conclusion holds: a black hole has a temperature.

T = ħc3 / (8πGMkB)

The temperature is inversely proportional to mass. A solar-mass black hole sits at about 60 billionths of a kelvin, far colder than the 2.7 K cosmic microwave background. It absorbs more than it emits and grows. Only a black hole lighter than about the Moon (around 4.5 × 1022 kg) is hot enough to lose mass today.

As it radiates, it loses mass, gets hotter, and radiates faster. The lifetime scales as the cube of the mass:

tevap ≈ 2 × 1067 yr × (M / M☉)3

For a solar-mass hole that is roughly 1057 times the current age of the universe. The final moments, by contrast, are explosive: the last few hundred tonnes go in about a second, in a flash of gamma rays. No such flash has been seen.

Hawking's result also gave black holes an entropy, proportional to the horizon's area rather than its volume (Bekenstein, 1973). That is strange for a thermodynamic object, and it is the root of the information paradox: if a black hole evaporates completely into thermal radiation, what happened to the detailed information about everything that fell in? Quantum mechanics says it cannot simply be erased. Work since 2019 on the "Page curve" suggests the information does come out, encoded in subtle correlations in the radiation, but how is still debated.
Thermal quanta leaving the horizon. For any astrophysical black hole the rate is absurdly small; this is not to scale in any dimension.
06  How we knowEvidence, not inference

Seen, heard,
and weighed.

For most of the twentieth century black holes were a prediction with no confirmed example. They are now routine observations.

  • 1916Karl Schwarzschild solves Einstein's equations for a point mass, weeks after general relativity is published, while serving on the Eastern Front.
  • 1939Oppenheimer and Snyder show that a sufficiently massive star must collapse past its own horizon.
  • 1971Cygnus X-1: an X-ray source orbiting a blue supergiant, with a companion too heavy to be a neutron star. The first strong candidate.
  • 1974Hawking publishes "Black hole explosions?", predicting that black holes radiate.
  • 2002–2018Genzel's and Ghez's teams track stars whipping around Sagittarius A* at thousands of km/s, weighing it at 4 million solar masses in a region smaller than the solar system.
  • 2015LIGO detects GW150914: gravitational waves from two black holes of 36 and 29 solar masses merging into one of 62. Three solar masses became energy in a fifth of a second.
  • 2019The Event Horizon Telescope releases the first image of a black hole's shadow, M87*, 55 million light-years away.
  • 2020Nobel Prize in Physics to Penrose (for proving collapse to a singularity is generic), Genzel and Ghez.
  • 2022EHT images Sagittarius A*. Its ring is the size predicted for 4 million solar masses to within a few percent.
